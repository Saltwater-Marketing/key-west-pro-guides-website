When most anglers hear the word seaweed, they immediately think about offshore dolphin (mahi-mahi) fishing. Around here, we're talking about sargassum, and under the right conditions, it's one of the best things you can find offshore.

A little bit is amazing.

A lot of it can create problems for just about everyone.

During a typical summer, we look for scattered weed lines moving west to east through the Gulf Stream at around two miles per hour. Those floating patches provide shade, attract baitfish, and become magnets for dolphin and other pelagic species. When the weed is spread out, it can mean fast-paced action and some incredible offshore fishing.

Unfortunately, that's not always the case.

For the past several years, we've seen massive amounts of sargassum, and when it piles up, it changes everything.

When Too Much Becomes Too Much

By the middle of summer, we usually enjoy light southeast winds, calm seas, warm water, and long beautiful days. It's also when the seaweed begins stacking offshore, along the beaches, and eventually washing ashore throughout the Keys.

We've always had seaweed, but in recent years the amount has become overwhelming.

As it collects on the beaches and begins to decompose, it releases hydrogen sulfide and ammonia, creating the familiar rotten egg smell that locals know all too well.

Offshore captains deal with another challenge. Instead of narrow weed lines that concentrate fish, they sometimes encounter mats nearly a quarter-mile wide. Those giant patches can make trolling difficult and can be nearly impossible to work around.

Why the Backcountry Stays So Fishable

Fortunately, the Florida Keys have a natural advantage.

The islands break apart much of the incoming seaweed and funnel it through the many channels that run between the islands. That helps keep large amounts of it from reaching much of the protected backcountry and the shallow Gulf waters.

Every morning, one of the first things we look at is the wind direction and the tide. Those two factors tell us where the cleanest water will be and help us avoid the largest concentrations of seaweed. Most days, with a little planning, we're able to fish areas with very little of it.

That's important because thick mats of seaweed can block sunlight from reaching the bottom and reduce oxygen levels in the water, both of which can impact these fragile ecosystems.

One of Key West's Best Summer Fisheries

Take a look at a map of Key West and you'll notice the expansive waters north of the islands. That's our backcountry, and it's one of the reasons summer fishing remains so productive even when offshore seaweed becomes a challenge.

The protected waters offer outstanding opportunities for a variety of species, including snapper, grouper, jacks, permit, barracuda, sharks, and tarpon. With short runs to the fishing grounds and easy access to fresh bait, we're able to spend more time fishing and less time running.

For many families and vacationers, a 4- or 5-hour backcountry trip is all it takes to enjoy a great day on the water. You'll experience beautiful scenery, steady fishing action, and if conditions allow, we'll even send you home with a few fresh fish for dinner.

Whether the offshore weed lines are perfect or piled high, one thing doesn't change—there's always a great place to fish in Key West. Knowing where to go each day is part of what makes fishing here so special.

If you're planning a trip this summer, our backcountry charters are a great way to enjoy productive fishing while staying in some of the most protected and scenic waters the Florida Keys have to offer.
