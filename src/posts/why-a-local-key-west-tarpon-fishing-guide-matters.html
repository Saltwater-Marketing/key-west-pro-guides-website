A Key West tarpon guide isn’t just someone who drives the boat.

During a sight-fishing trip, your captain is constantly reading the water, looking for fish and making decisions based on what’s happening around Key West that day.

That’s especially important during the spring and early-summer tarpon migration.

These are moving fish.

Finding them, positioning the boat and creating a good casting opportunity takes local knowledge. And when you’re staring across clear water trying to pick out a Silver King, there’s a good chance your guide is going to see it before you do.

Key West Tarpon Fishing Is a Visual Game

One of the experiences that defines our Key West tarpon fishery is sight-casting to migrating fish in clear water.

The captain may be looking for a rolling fish, a tail, dark back or flash beneath the surface.

When something appears, the boat has to get into position.

That’s where experience comes in.

The captain is thinking about where the fish is moving and where you need to make the presentation.

Meanwhile, the angler needs to be ready.

Polarized sunglasses help, but don’t worry if you don’t immediately see what your captain sees. Professional guides spend far more time reading these waters than visiting anglers do.

Listen to the direction you’re given.

Sometimes making the cast when you’re told matters more than seeing the fish yourself.

Knowing Key West Water

The shallow-water fishery around Key West isn’t one uniform stretch of water.

Our backcountry includes islands, basins, channels, creeks and shallow waters. Farther west, the Marquesas offer crystal-clear flats, channels and mangroves.

Tarpon move through this larger fishery, and conditions change.

A local guide isn’t simply running to the same coordinates every morning. They’re using experience to decide where and how to fish based on the opportunities available.

Your Guide Helps With More Than Location

Finding the fish is only part of the job.

Your captain also helps you make the presentation.

Key West Pro Guides targets tarpon with flies, live bait and plugs. Documented live bait options include mullet and pinfish, while fly anglers can use streamer flies.

If you’re sight-casting to moving fish, where that presentation lands matters.

Then there’s what happens when a tarpon eats.

These fish have exceptionally hard mouths, so a solid hook-set is important. Your captain can explain what to do before that moment arrives.

Instruction is included with every trip.
